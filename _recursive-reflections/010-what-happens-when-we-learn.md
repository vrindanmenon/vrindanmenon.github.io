> "Tell me and I forget. Teach me and I remember. Involve me and I
> learn." --- Chinese proverb

For a long time, I thought I had learnt quite a lot in school. But when
I look back, I realize there is a huge difference between what I
remember and what I actually learnt. We accumulated so much information
--- disconnected, incomplete facts that couldn't be applied anywhere
other than on an examination paper.

Albert Einstein, during his address to the State University of New York
in 1936, quoted: "Education is that which remains, if one has forgotten
everything he learned in school."

If that is true, what remains in me?

When I look at the moments where I have genuinely learnt something,
whether it was decoding Mathematics, understanding Physics and Nature,
or simply watching my son figure out the world, I often see six
different elements appear most of the time:

-   **Context** --- Where does this idea come from?
-   **Connection** --- Does it connect to what I already know?
-   **Visualization** --- Can I actually see or imagine it?
-   **Practice** --- Can I use it and work with it?
-   **Need** --- Do I have a reason to know this?
-   **Relevance** --- Why does this matter to me?

Are these six separate parts of learning? Or are they just different
ways of creating a relationship between the learner and what is being
learnt?

But how can knowledge in a curriculum become something a child needs,
rather than something they are told to learn? Can we connect new
knowledge to something the child already understands or cares about? Can
curiosity itself provide the context and relevance for learning?

I often think about this while exploring the night sky. I can spend
hours trying to understand what I am seeing, why it appears that way,
imagining possibilities and creating my own stories around it. For most
of us nowadays, astronomy gives a sense of awe and a glimpse of how vast
the world beyond our immediate experience really is, rather than
"usefulness". Modern societal structures have made much of this
knowledge feel distant from everyday life. We no longer need to navigate
by the stars when Google Maps can guide us, or read the seasons to know
when to sow and harvest when our food comes from markets.

Yet, it still quenches a deeper thirst: it forces us to look outward at
reality and grasp the scale of how small we are in the vastness of the
Universe. It is deeply meaningful.

This made me question the way I think about relevance. Relevance does
not always have to come from usefulness. Something can be relevant
simply because it helps me understand something I am curious about or
changes the way I see the world. Astronomy may not help me solve an
immediate problem, but my curiosity about it gives me a reason to keep
learning.

## Knowledge as a Lens

What is different inside me when I have genuinely learnt something? Not
what did the teacher do? Not what should schools do? But what changes in
me?

When learning is considered as "acquiring information", it becomes:

-   I know this.
-   I can remember this.
-   I can reproduce this when asked.

But when we consider learning as "changing what I can see", it becomes:

-   I notice something I didn't notice before.
-   I make a connection I couldn't make before.
-   I ask a different question.
-   I can use an idea in a new situation.
-   The world looks slightly different to me.

When learning happens this way, knowledge becomes a lens.

Before learning about light waves, I simply looked at the world, taking
every shadow and brightness for granted. But after I learnt more about
light, I could use what I understood to interpret the optical phenomena
happening all around me. Before mathematics, I saw patterns around me,
but I never thought of them as mathematics. When I started learning it
differently, I began seeing those patterns everywhere in nature, in
numbers, and in everyday things.

Learning is not just adding something to what I know. It changes what I
am able to notice, understand, connect, or do.

As Doris Lessing wrote, "That is what learning is. You suddenly
understand something you've understood all your life, but in a new way."

There are two ways we could think about this process.

-   Sometimes learning begins with knowledge. I learn an idea, connect
    it to something I already know, and gradually begin to understand it
    differently.
-   Sometimes it begins with an experience. I encounter something I
    don't understand, or I get stuck trying to do something. That
    creates a need to know. I seek the knowledge, try it out, and my
    understanding changes.

John Dewey's ideas are particularly relevant to that second process. For
Dewey, an experience becomes genuinely educational when we don't merely
have the experience, but begin to investigate it. He described learning
as emerging through an experimental relationship between what we do and
what happens as a result.

Dewey placed the "problematic situation" at the very beginning of
inquiry. I learn something deeply when I try to do something
practically, figure out that I am stuck, and realize I need to gain a
specific skill to move forward. His inquiry model requires forming a
problem, developing explanations, reasoning through them, and testing
them through action.

Seymour Papert takes this a step further with his theory of
Constructionism, which proposes that learners develop understanding
through actively making things in the world, not merely receiving
information.

But suppose a child makes something, experiments, fails, fixes it, and
eventually gets it working. Have they necessarily learnt deeply?

Not necessarily. The missing link, as pointed out by research
surrounding constructionist learning at MIT's Media Lab, is reflection.
Reflecting on the "how" and "why" of a design process is what turns an
activity into integrated understanding. It is where experience and
knowledge interact so that information finally becomes true
understanding.

## The Questions We Must Ask

If learning is this complex, beautiful, and deeply personal alchemy of
experience, connection, action, and reflection, then I find myself
wondering what kind of education can make room for it. We cannot stop at
the curriculum and lesson plan. We also have to think deeply about the
conditions in which learning happens --- whether a child has the time
and freedom to question, explore, make, get stuck, and discover.

As I read Dewey's ideas about problematic situations, Papert's ideas
about making, and Krishnamurti's questions about fear and conformity in
education, I started to reflect on some of the assumptions we make about
learning.

What happens when we look at education through these questions?

-   **On Need and Relevance:** If a child has a genuine need to know,
    can we create enough space for them to encounter the question before
    we give them the answer? How do we create experiences that invite
    curiosity rather than simply deliver conclusions?
-   **On the Head, Hand, and Heart:** If learning can involve thinking,
    doing and feeling, what happens when most of learning happens at a
    desk? What might change if the hands, the body and the natural world
    had a larger place in learning?
-   **On Fear and Intelligence:** Jiddu Krishnamurti observed that, "Any
    form of compulsion, whether through reward or punishment, breeds
    fear, and fear destroys intelligence." What happens to learning when
    a child is afraid of being wrong? Do we create enough space for
    children to make mistakes, get stuck and try again without
    immediately worrying about being judged?
-   **On Time and Discovery:** What happens to curiosity when there is
    always a next lesson to get to? How do we leave room for a child to
    linger with a question, especially when the most interesting
    learning may not happen within the planned lesson?
-   **On Community:** If learning is also social and interconnected,
    what do we lose when so much of schooling is organised around
    individual achievement and comparison?

Ultimately, we cannot design a space for true learning if we have lost
touch with what it feels like to learn. To find the answer, I had to ask
myself:

-   What made some things I learnt become part of how I see the world,
    while other things remained just information I could reproduce?
-   What could I see, understand, question or do after learning that I
    couldn't before?
-   When I think back to my own school years, what do I actually
    remember learning?
-   What happens between being taught something and making it my own?
-   When I encounter something I do not understand, do I feel the open
    space of curiosity, or do I feel the anxiety of needing to be
    "right"?
-   Do I give myself the permission to linger in confusion, to make
    mistakes, and to slowly figure things out?
-   If learning is a shift in perspective, what am I willing to unlearn?
-   If education is what remains after we have forgotten everything we
    were taught, then what is left inside me?

Through these questions, I have begun to understand what learning means
to me. Learning isn't about reaching a destination or acquiring a set of
facts. It is simply the lifelong, quiet process of becoming more
observant, more connected, and more awake to the world around me.

The inquiry continues.
