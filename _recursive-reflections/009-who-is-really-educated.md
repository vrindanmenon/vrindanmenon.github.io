I started with a much simpler question: **what actually is learning?** How do I know I am learning something? What enables it to happen? For me, if I want to design learning spaces, I think I first have to answer these questions.

Learning has many formal definitions. The [Oxford Learner’s Dictionary](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/learn) defines *learn* as gaining knowledge or skill through studying, experience or being taught. The Britannica said “activity or process of gaining knowledge or skill by studying, practicing, being taught or experiencing some”. 

Hmm… When I look back at my own education, I can remember many things I was taught. Formulas, definitions, dates, methods. I struggled to reproduce many of them when I needed to, especially when I was preparing for an examination. **Even worse, how much of what I learnt do I actually know how to use today? How much of it can I use to understand something new, solve a problem, make a decision or connect it with something else I already know?**

I looked at how learning begins for a child. For a baby learning to move, there is no separate lesson called “learning to walk”. The child moves, reaches, pulls up, falls, tries again and over time discovers what their body can do. Language develops through experience too. A child hears sounds, makes sounds, gets responses from the people around them, begins to associate sounds with people and things, and gradually builds language.

What interested me here was the relationship between **the child and the environment**. The child brings certain capacities, but what happens to those capacities depends greatly on experience and interaction. The old nature–nurture question becomes relevant here. It's not one or the other. Development happens through the interaction between what the child brings and the environment in which the child grows. Research on early development shows the importance of responsive, back-and-forth interactions with adults in supporting development.

At around three or four, we move children into an organised system where learning happens within a structure designed by someone else. There is a timetable, a curriculum and an assessment for almost everything. The child is still learning, of course. But we have taken away much of that freedom by deciding when, what and for how long they should learn. And every year, the system that claims to educate the child becomes more structured.

I keep wondering **what changes when learning becomes an organised activity rather than something so closely connected to living.** The child is still learning, but now the learning has to fit but now the learning has to fit into a mould designed by the system.

I have seen this especially with mathematics. A student can confidently use a rule, but struggle to explain why it works. They have learnt how to perform the procedure, but the mathematical idea itself has not necessarily become something they can reason with. I have also seen students encounter a pattern or a situation and gradually arrive at an explanation themselves. **Knowing a procedure and understanding an idea are not necessarily the same thing. An important part of learning is being able to observe what is happening and reason with what we observe, rather than simply knowing which rule to apply.**

When I say I learnt history, physics, mathematics or languages, what exactly do I mean? I remember studying them. I remember writing answers and preparing for examinations. But much of what I memorised disappeared afterwards. 

I used to think of this as the brain simply “clearing the cache”. Forgetting is a normal part of memory, and retrieving something helps us remember it for longer. But learning is not simply retrieving information either. What we already know plays a major role in what we are able to learn next. When we encounter something new, we make sense of it through what we already know - we connect, organise, question, and sometimes use it to understand something else.

That makes me think differently about what has stayed with me. Some ideas I learnt long ago have become part of how I think and work, while others have faded away. What makes the difference?

This is also making me rethink what I mean when I say that children should be free to learn. I don't think freedom means leaving a child alone to discover everything. Children need knowledge, language, people, materials, explanations, practice, feedback and challenges. **Sometimes they need to be shown something directly. Sometimes they need the time and space to discover it for themselves.**

What interests me is where those two meet: **How can the concepts and ideas in the curriculum that we introduce to a child become something they want to explore, question and understand for themselves?**

Earlier I was thinking about what I should put into a learning space — a workshop, a lab, books, technology, mathematical objects, nature and projects. Now I am more interested in what the space enables.

**How can I create a space where the child has a reason to ask the question, so that the knowledge in the curriculum becomes something they need rather than something they are told to learn? How can learning become something joyful and multi-layered rather than something that mainly happens by sitting still and receiving information?**

I don't have a clear understanding yet. But looking back, one thing is clearer to me: **education should leave us with a greater capacity to notice, understand, do and become. Otherwise, it risks becoming little more than an exercise in memorisation.**"

And I think I need to keep sitting with the original question: **what does it actually mean to say that someone is educated? I started with one question and somehow ended up with several more. That seems to be becoming a pattern.**
