I sometimes wonder if we have become too used to being told what to do,
that we have forgotten how to figure things out ourselves.

We wait to be told what to do, what the right answer is, or whether we
have done well enough. Even when we have an idea of our own, we look
around for reassurance before taking the next step.

I notice this in myself very often. Having an idea and acting on it are
not always the same thing. It makes me wonder, how much of this has to
do with the way we learn?

When we think about education, we often ask what children are learning
and how well they are learning it. But do we also ask whether they are
learning to think for themselves and find their own way?

What happens when a child is taught one way to solve a problem and
hesitates to try a different approach? What happens when they have a
question but hesitate to ask it? What happens when they know something
isn't making sense but assume the explanation must be right because an
adult gave it?

I have been thinking about these things while reading John Dewey and
Jiddu Krishnamurti.

Dewey made me think about the kind of structure that helps a learner
become independent, rather than dependent on instructions. Krishnamurti
made me think about what happens when we are constantly told what to
think, what to do and what to follow.

I don't think that means children should be left to figure everything
out on their own. They need guidance, practice, and someone to help when
they get stuck. But there is a huge difference between guiding a child
towards understanding and teaching them a method to follow to arrive at
a conclusion without proper understanding.

It becomes clear when we look at how we respond to mistakes.

When a child makes a mistake, do we help them understand what happened?
Or do we make them so conscious of getting things right that they stop
exploring ideas they aren't sure about?

I remember how I used to get scolded and punished for making mistakes,
and most of the time it made me feel inadequate. Looking back, I wonder
what a child takes away from these experiences. When does a child become
so afraid of making mistakes that they stop trying different approaches
or looking at things from a different perspective?

**Krishnamurti writes in *On Education*, in the chapter "On Freedom and
Order":**

> "When you are told what to do, what to think, to obey, to follow, do
> you know what it does to you? Your mind becomes dull, it loses its
> initiative, its quickness."

Krishnamurti also makes an important distinction between freedom and
simply doing whatever we want:

> "Freedom does not exist without order. The two go together. If you
> cannot have order, you cannot have freedom."

He goes on to describe how consideration, thoughtfulness and
watchfulness towards others can bring about order. This is different
from simply obeying rules because someone has imposed them.

It makes me wonder whether we can help children develop this kind of
understanding through their everyday experiences. Can they learn to
recognize how their actions affect others, take responsibility for their
choices and understand why certain things matter, rather than merely
follow instructions?

They might know how to follow instructions, complete tasks and meet
expectations. But will they be willing to question something that
doesn't make sense? Try a different approach? Begin something when there
is no clear path to follow?

**Freedom in learning is not simply about allowing children to do what
they want. It is about helping them develop the ability to think, make
decisions, learn from mistakes and consider how their actions affect
others.**

**That kind of freedom needs an environment. It needs opportunities to
explore, problems worth thinking about, room to get things wrong, and
adults who can offer guidance without taking over every step.**

**It also needs structure. Not rules imposed from outside, but an
understanding of why certain things matter and how we live and learn
alongside others.**

**Dewey offers another perspective in *Experience and Education*:**

> "Experience and education cannot be directly equated to each other.
> For some experiences are mis-educative."

He explains further:

> "Any experience is mis-educative that has the effect of arresting or
> distorting the growth of further experience. An experience may be such
> as to engender callousness; it may produce lack of sensitivity, and of
> responsiveness. Then the possibilities of having richer experience in
> the future are restricted."

He explains that some experiences can limit our ability to learn from
what comes next. A person might become skilled at doing something in one
particular way, and then that limits them from exploring other
approaches. Even experiences that are enjoyable can leave us with habits
that prevent us from learning more deeply.

This made me pause.

Dewey and Krishnamurti have made me look at education a little
differently. They made me think beyond what a child learns, more towards
the experiences that shape how they learn, think and act.

**Education cannot be measured only by what a child can do when someone
shows them the way. The most important measure is to see how the child
responds when the way is unclear, when an answer doesn't come easily, or
when they have to make a decision for themselves.**

Do they wait for someone to tell them what to do? Are they afraid of
getting it wrong? Or can they stay with the problem, question what they
know, try another approach and keep going?

But where does that confidence come from? What makes one child see a
difficult problem as something worth exploring?

I want to explore this further in my next article. What happens when
children struggle with a difficult problem? What makes them stay with
it, try different approaches, and experience the joy of finally figuring
it out? And how do these experiences shape what they believe about their
own abilities?
