I didn't start thinking about education because I wanted to redesign education.

I started with mathematics.

I wanted to understand why something I had come to find so interesting often became something students wanted to get over with as quickly as possible.

The irony is that mathematics wasn't something I particularly enjoyed in school either. My school years were not a time when I felt connected to learning. There were a few concepts I enjoyed, but for the most part, I learnt because I had to. College changed something for me, and much later, my work brought me back to mathematics in a completely different way.

At work, I became interested in the storytelling side of mathematics. I enjoyed taking an idea that looked complicated and breaking it down into smaller questions. Instead of beginning with the method, I wanted to find the question that could lead us towards the method.

I enjoyed this process more than simply explaining mathematics. There was something satisfying about taking an idea apart, finding the smaller questions underneath it, and then putting it back together in a way that made sense. It was this journey towards understanding mathematics that slowly took me on a longer journey of trying to make learning meaningful.

My son played another major role in this journey. Learning along with him made me relearn many things myself, and it brought a kind of joy and meaning to learning that I hadn't experienced before. His questions often make me stop and think. Sometimes they are questions I wouldn't have thought to ask myself.

I never want a system to take that away from him. More than that, I don't want us to build systems that slowly take it away from children in general. I believe that curiosity is already there in every child. What we need to do is nurture it, keep that fire alive, and give it room to grow brighter.

That is where my interest in mathematics became something larger. I was no longer only interested in how to make a mathematical idea easier to understand. I was becoming interested in how we can protect that desire to understand, to really learn, in the first place.

I don't know yet what education should look like to enable real learning. But I know I don't want to design something that makes a child stop asking questions just because the curriculum has moved on. That is the question I am carrying forward: **how do we build learning environments that don't just teach children more, but help them keep wanting to know more?**
