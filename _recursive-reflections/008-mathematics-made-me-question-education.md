I didn't start thinking about education because I wanted to redesign education.

I started with mathematics.

I wanted to understand why something I had come to find so interesting often became something students wanted to get over with as quickly as possible.

The irony is that mathematics wasn’t something I particularly enjoyed in school either. My school years were not a time when I felt connected to learning. There were a few concepts I enjoyed, but for the most part, I learnt because I had to. College changed something for me, and much later, my work gave me a new perspective for mathematics. 

I became interested in the storytelling side of mathematics. I enjoyed taking an idea that looked complicated and breaking it down into smaller questions. Instead of beginning with the method, I wanted to find the question that could lead us towards the method.

There was something satisfying about taking an idea apart, finding the smaller questions underneath it, and then putting it back together in a way that made sense. It was this journey towards understanding mathematics that slowly took me on a longer journey of trying to make learning meaningful.

My son played another major role in this journey. Learning along with him made me unlearn and relearn many things myself, and it brought a kind of joy and meaning to learning that I hadn’t experienced before. It also made me look at things with a child's curiosity and innocence, rather than always looking at them through the ideas I already knew (what I thought I knew). His questions often make me stop and think. Sometimes they are questions I wouldn’t have thought to ask myself.

And that got me thinking - what happens when this wonderfully curious little person meets a wonderfully organized education system? I never want a system to take that away from him. And I don’t want other children to lose it either. It motivated me to research and work towards designing learning environments that keep that curiosity alive and give it room to grow. 

I don’t know yet what education should look like to enable real learning. But I don’t want to design something that makes a child stop asking questions just because the curriculum has moved on or stop him from trying because making a mistake is seen as a mistake. 

**Learning needs a little more room for questions, wrong turns and the more “Oops!” than “Yay!”.** And that is what I am carrying forward: **how do we build learning environments that don’t just teach children more, but help them keep wanting to know more?**
