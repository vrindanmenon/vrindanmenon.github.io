I started with a much simpler question: **what actually is learning?** How do I know I am learning something? What enables it to happen? For me, if I want to design learning spaces, I think I first have to answer these questions.

Learning has many definitions. The [Oxford Learner’s Dictionary](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/learn) defines *learn* as gaining knowledge or skill through studying, experience or being taught. It also describes learning through experience. 

When I look back at my own education, I can remember many things I was taught. Formulas, definitions, dates, methods. I struggled to reproduce many of them when I needed to, especially when I was preparing for an examination. 

**But how much of what I learnt do I actually know how to use today? How much of it can I use to understand something new? To solve a problem? To make a decision? To make something? To connect it with something else I already know?**

This made me think about the difference between being able to reproduce something and being able to use what we have learnt.

I looked at how learning begins for a child. Think about a baby learning to move. There is no separate lesson called “learning to walk”. The child moves, reaches, pulls up, falls, tries again and gradually discovers what their body can do. Language develops through experience too. A child hears sounds, makes sounds, gets responses from the people around them, begins to associate sounds with people and things, and gradually builds language. What interested me here was the relationship between **the child and the environment**. The child brings certain capacities, but what happens to those capacities depends greatly on experience and interaction. This is where the old nature–nurture question becomes relevant to learning: it isn't one or the other. Development happens through the interaction between what the child brings and the environment in which the child grows. Research on early development also shows how important responsive, back-and-forth interactions with adults are in supporting language, social development and the developing brain.


Then we move children into an organised system. At around three or four, learning begins to happen within a structure designed by someone else. There is a timetable, curriculum, subjects, lessons, worksheets, assessments and grades. And every year, the system that claims to educate the child becomes more structured. I keep wondering what changes when learning becomes an organised activity rather than something so closely connected to living. The child is still learning, but now the learning has to fit into a sequence, a period of time and eventually an assessment.

At some point, **the evidence of learning can become more important than the learning itself.** Can you read this? Can you write this? Can you solve this? Can you remember this? Can you reproduce it in an examination? I have seen this particularly clearly in mathematics. A student can confidently use a rule such as \(x^a \times x^b = x^{a+b}\), but struggle to explain why it works. They have learnt how to perform the procedure, but the mathematical idea itself has not necessarily become something they can reason with. I have also seen students encounter a pattern or a situation and gradually arrive at an explanation themselves. That distinction has stayed with me. **Knowing a procedure and understanding an idea are not necessarily the same thing.**

When I say I learnt history, physics, mathematics or languages, what exactly do I mean? I remember studying them. I remember writing answers and preparing for examinations. But much of what I memorised disappeared afterwards. I used to think of this as the brain simply “clearing the cache”. I don't think that is quite right anymore. Forgetting is a normal part of memory, and retrieving information is important for retaining it. But learning is not simply retrieving information either. What we already know plays a major role in what we are able to learn next. New knowledge is interpreted through existing knowledge, and learning becomes more meaningful when we can connect, organise, explain and use what we have encountered. The National Academies' *How People Learn* describes learners as coming to new situations with prior knowledge, skills, beliefs and concepts that influence what they notice, how they interpret it, what they remember and how they reason. It also describes learning as involving transfer from previous experiences.


Retrieval practice itself also has strong evidence behind it for improving later retention, but retrieval is one part of a larger learning process.


That makes me think differently about the things that have actually stayed with me. Some things I learnt in school have continued to be useful, but often not in the way they were originally taught. An idea I learnt in one context gets connected to something else much later. Sometimes I remember the idea itself; sometimes I don't remember where I learnt it, but I recognise it when I encounter it again. Some knowledge becomes useful because I keep using it, some because I connect it with something new, and some probably stays dormant until I encounter a situation where I need it.

Other things I once studied have become only fragments of memory. I can vaguely remember learning them, but I cannot do much with them now. That doesn't necessarily mean that I never learnt them or that they were useless. It makes me wonder what happens between learning something for the first time and being able to use it much later.

Maybe this is why simply asking whether a child has learnt something is not enough. We also need to ask what the child can do with it afterwards — whether they can recognise it in a new situation, connect it with something they already know, use it to understand something else, or build something from it.

This is also making me rethink what I mean when I say that children should be free to learn. I don't think freedom means leaving a child alone to discover everything. Children need knowledge, language, people, materials, explanations, practice, feedback and challenges. Sometimes they need to be shown something directly. Sometimes they need the time and space to discover it for themselves. The interesting question for me is where those two meet: **what needs to be taught, what can be discovered, and what kind of environment allows the child to make something of what they encounter?**

This is where my thinking about learning spaces has started to change. Earlier I was thinking about what I should put into a learning space — a workshop, a lab, books, technology, mathematical objects, nature and projects. Now I am more interested in what the space enables. Can a child encounter something that doesn't make sense? Can they stay with it? Can they find something to manipulate? Can they ask another person? Can they make something? Can they fail and return to the same idea? Can something they learnt in mathematics become useful while building something in a workshop? Can they spend three hours following one question even though the timetable says they should have moved on after forty minutes? These are becoming more important questions for me than simply deciding what subjects the space should contain.

And this brings me back to the question I started with: **who is really educated?** Is it the person who remembers the most, the person with the most qualifications, or the person who can take what they know and make sense of something they have never encountered before? Someone who can learn without always being taught? Someone who can recognise when they don't understand? Someone who can connect one idea with another, change their mind when the evidence changes, and use knowledge to make decisions, create things, solve problems and participate meaningfully in the world around them?

I don't have a clear understanding yet. But looking back one thing is clearer to me: **education should leave us with more than what we were able to reproduce while we were inside the system. It should change what we are capable of noticing, understanding, doing and becoming afterwards.**

And I think I need to keep sitting with the original question: **what does it actually mean to say that someone is educated?**
