I started with a much simpler question: **what actually is learning?** How do I know I am learning something? What enables it to happen? For me, if I want to design learning spaces, I think I first have to answer these questions.

Learning has many formal definitions. The [Oxford Learner’s Dictionary](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/learn) defines *learn* as gaining knowledge or skill through studying, experience or being taught. The Britannica said “activity or process of gaining knowledge or skill by studying, practicing, being taught or experiencing some”. 

Hmm… When I look back at my own education, I can remember many things I was taught. Formulas, definitions, dates, methods. I struggled to reproduce many of them when I needed to, especially when I was preparing for an examination.

**But how much of what I learnt do I actually know how to use today? How much of it can I use to understand something new, solve a problem, make a decision or connect it with something else I already know?**

I looked at how learning begins for a child. Think about a baby learning to move. There is no separate lesson called “learning to walk”. The child moves, reaches, pulls up, falls, tries again and gradually discovers what their body can do. Language develops through experience too. A child hears sounds, makes sounds, gets responses from the people around them, begins to associate sounds with people and things, and gradually builds language.

What interested me here was the relationship between **the child and the environment**. The child brings certain capacities, but what happens to those capacities depends greatly on experience and interaction. The old nature–nurture question becomes relevant here. It isn't one or the other. Development happens through the interaction between what the child brings and the environment in which the child grows. Research on early development also shows the importance of responsive, back-and-forth interactions with adults in supporting development.

Then we move children into an organised system. At around three or four, learning begins to happen within a structure designed by someone else. There is a timetable, curriculum, subjects, lessons, worksheets, assessments and grades. And every year, the system that claims to educate the child becomes more structured.

I keep wondering **what changes when learning becomes an organised activity rather than something so closely connected to living.** The child is still learning, but now the learning has to fit into a sequence, a period of time and eventually an assessment.

I have seen this particularly clearly in mathematics. A student can confidently use a rule, but struggle to explain why it works. They have learnt how to perform the procedure, but the mathematical idea itself has not necessarily become something they can reason with. I have also seen students encounter a pattern or a situation and gradually arrive at an explanation themselves.

**Knowing a procedure and understanding an idea are not necessarily the same thing.**

When I say I learnt history, physics, mathematics or languages, what exactly do I mean? I remember studying them. I remember writing answers and preparing for examinations. But much of what I memorised disappeared afterwards. 

I used to think of this as the brain simply “clearing the cache”. I don't think that is quite right anymore. Forgetting is a normal part of memory, and retrieving information is important for retaining it. But learning is not simply retrieving information either. What we already know plays a major role in what we are able to learn next. **New knowledge is interpreted through existing knowledge, and becomes more meaningful when we can connect, organise, explain and use it.**

That makes me think differently about the things that have stayed with me. Some things I learnt in school have continued to be useful, but often not in the way they were originally taught. An idea I learnt in one context gets connected to something else much later. Sometimes I remember the idea itself; sometimes I don't remember where I learnt it, but I recognise it when I encounter it again.

Other things I once studied have become only fragments of memory. I cannot do much with them now. It makes me wonder what happens between learning something for the first time and being able to use it much later.

This is also making me rethink what I mean when I say that children should be free to learn. I don't think freedom means leaving a child alone to discover everything. Children need knowledge, language, people, materials, explanations, practice, feedback and challenges. **Sometimes they need to be shown something directly. Sometimes they need the time and space to discover it for themselves.**

The interesting question for me is where those two meet: **what needs to be taught, what can be discovered, and what kind of environment allows the child to make something of what they encounter?**

This is where my thinking about learning spaces has started to change. Earlier I was thinking about what I should put into a learning space — a workshop, a lab, books, technology, mathematical objects, nature and projects. Now I am more interested in what the space enables.

**How can I create a space where the child has a reason to ask the question, so that the knowledge in the curriculum becomes something they need rather than something they are told to learn? How can learning become something joyful and multi-layered rather than something that mainly happens by sitting still and receiving information?**

These are becoming more important questions for me than simply deciding what the space should contain.

I don't have a clear understanding yet. But looking back, one thing is clearer to me: education should change what we are capable of noticing, understanding, doing and becoming afterwards. Otherwise it’s just a brain exercise in memorisation.

And I think I need to keep sitting with the original question: **what does it actually mean to say that someone is educated?**
